The Amber Dress Problem

I built an AI wardrobe that cuts your clothes out of any photo and renders them worn on a mannequin. The hard part wasn't the AI — it was catching the AI when it lied.

I asked an image model to show me a yellow dress being worn.

It gave me an amber one. Slightly deeper, slightly more orange — the kind of difference you would not notice unless the dress were yours. It was mine, in the sense that I had photographed it and put it in the app. So I noticed.

The interesting part is why it happened, and it was not the model's fault. My own code had told it to make an amber dress. I'll come back to that.

The problem nobody has solved for you

Most people own more clothes than they can hold in their head. The consequences are boring and constant: the same three outfits on rotation, garments bought and never worn, and a decision every morning made against a wardrobe you cannot see all of at once.

The existing answers all miss:

  • A photo album is not a wardrobe. You cannot ask it what goes together.
  • Retail try-on styles you in clothes you don't own. That's advertising, not help.
  • Every "digitise your closet" app starts with photograph each garment against a plain white background. Nobody does that. That instruction is where those apps die.

So the real constraint is this: the input has to be the photos people already have. A screenshot from a shop. A flat lay on a bed. A picture of your friend wearing the thing at a party, with a lamp behind her and her hair over one shoulder.

If you can solve that, you have a wardrobe. If you can't, you have a chore.

What I built

Pink Petal Closet — live app · source

Give it any clothing photograph. It returns just that garment, cut out on transparency — background gone, model gone, hanger gone. Those cutouts become a browsable closet. Then you type where you are going, in plain words, and it assembles an outfit from your own clothes. Press one button and it returns a photograph of that outfit actually being worn.

Three pieces of work, in order of difficulty:

1. Get the garment out. The hard one. Vision-language model, human parsing, watershed segmentation, occlusion repair.

2. Decide what to wear. Fourteen occasion buckets, tag scoring, colour harmony, dress-versus-separates.

3. Show it. A flat display for free; an AI render on demand, verified before it's trusted.

The Google Cloud stack: Vertex AI, Gemini API, Cloud Run, Cloud Build, Artifact Registry, Secret Manager, Cloud Storage, Cloud Logging, IAM.

Two deliberate choices worth naming:

The server has zero npm dependencies. Node's standard library only. The build is a COPY. There is no lockfile to drift and no third-party code in the one process that holds the credential.

Renders need no key at all. On Cloud Run the container asks the metadata server for a token for its own service account. Nothing to paste, expire, or rotate. The API key that does exist lives in Secret Manager and arrives as an environment variable — never in the image, never in the repo, never in the page.

Implementation, step by step

1. Ask the picture what it is

Before touching a pixel, a vision-language model answers two questions no pixel rule can: which garment is this photo about, and where is it?

This matters more than it sounds. A photo of an outfit contains four garments. A shop screenshot contains a garment and a "Add to bag" button. Without this step you get a confident cutout of the wrong thing.

2. Separate clothing from body

MediaPipe's selfie_multiclass_256x256 runs in the browser, in WASM, cached after first load. It labels every pixel: background, hair, skin, face, clothes, accessory.

This is the single best decision in the project. Human parsing is the expensive part of the problem and it costs zero API calls, forever.

3. Flood from the backdrop, not the colour

Here is the case that kills colour-threshold approaches: a white top on a white sweep. There is no colour difference to threshold.

So instead: marker-based watershed segmentation (Meyer's flooding) from the backdrop inward, with an MSER-style stability sweep over 16 candidate thresholds — pick the segmentation that changes least as you vary the threshold, because that's the one the image actually supports.

Measured cutout quality: 94–100 on clean product shots, 76–81 on white-on-white. Not perfect. Previously unusable.

4. Put back some of what the hair was covering

A photo of someone wearing a dress has a bite missing where her hair falls over her shoulder. Nobody wants a dress with a bite in it.

The approach: morphologically close the garment mask, look at each resulting notch, and ask whether an occluder is standing in it. Fill only those.

This works less often than I would like, and it's worth being precise about why — because the honest version is more interesting than the pitch.

Closing only bridges a gap it can span, and it only finds notches that fabric encloses. So the repair handles a lock of hair lying across the middle of a chest, and does nothing at all for hair falling over the outer edge of a shoulder — because that notch opens to the background, and a notch open to the background is indistinguishable from a genuine gap in the outfit. The space between two trouser legs. The split of a wrap skirt. Those must not be painted in, and the guard that protects them is the same guard that refuses the hair.

Measured on five real photographs of people:

dress_dark_runway     1388 px repaired   5.2% of the garment
dress_long_1997       3924 px repaired   3.2%
dress_red_runway       164 px repaired   0.7%   (invisible)
dress_white_outdoor       0 px           nothing
casual_tshirt_jeans       0 px           nothing

why notches were thrown away:  6x "mostly background"
                               2x "it's the garment's own neckline"
                               1x "no occluder explains it"

Two out of five, meaningfully. I shipped it because a repaired bite is strictly better than a bitten one and the failure mode is "unchanged" rather than "wrong" — but the docs I first wrote said fabric behind hair "is reconstructed", full stop, and that was a claim my own measurements didn't support. Someone using the app told me so before I caught it myself, which is the least comfortable way to find out.

The fix I won't ship without more evidence: loosening the open-to-the-background guard. It would catch the shoulder case and it would also start inventing fabric between trouser legs. Painting cloth that was never there is a worse failure than leaving a notch, so that guard stays until I can tell the two cases apart properly.

5. Matte the edge

Soft alpha at the boundary, plus colour decontamination — because a pixel that is 60% dress and 40% background carries background colour into your garment, and a hundred of those give the cutout a halo.

6. Score it, and admit when it's bad

Every cutout gets a quality number. A bad one is flagged rather than shipped silently. There's a Cutout Editor for the rest: tap the garment you want, drag a box, or fix the edge by hand with three brushes — Erase, Draw Back (restores the photograph's own pixels) and Fill In (invents fabric from the garment around the gap).

7. Read the occasion like a person would

You type "diwali dinner". The stylist sorts it into one of fourteen buckets — party, casual, work, date, edgy, beach, autumn, formal, active, cold, rain, travel, festive, sombre — then scores every garment on tag fit plus colour harmony against anything you've pinned.

It picks between a dress and separates by scoring the best dress against the best top-and-bottom pair. And when it doesn't recognise a phrase, it says so instead of quietly returning something generic. An honest "I don't know this one" is worth more than a confident wrong answer.

8. Show the pieces before spending money

Before any render, the look appears as a flat display of your real cutouts laid out on white.

This was originally a mannequin. I took it out, and it's one of my favourite decisions in the project. Placing a flat photograph on a body invites a comparison it cannot win — every place the fabric fails to follow the shoulder reads as a defect in your garment. A flat lay promises nothing about drape, so it can't break that promise.

9. Render — then check the render

gemini-2.5-flash-image gets the cutouts and a bare mannequin, and returns a photograph of the outfit worn.

Then the result is verified against your own photo. Colour distance in CIE Lab; length as height over shoulder width. If the model changed your garment, the render is refused and your photo is kept. On a faithful render: ΔE 1.4 and 0.1% length drift, against thresholds of 12 and 20%.

Every render that passes is stored in IndexedDB keyed to that exact set of garments. Returning to a look is instant and free. It's uncapped on purpose — localStorage's 5 MB forced eviction, which meant a render you had paid for could vanish because you'd rendered others since. Wrong behaviour for something bought.

Five bugs that taught me something

This is the part I'd actually want to read, so here it is.

The amber dress: I promoted a lossy label to ground truth

The render prompt said "an amber dress". Because my code looked up the garment's stored colour name — and that name came from a coarse 20-bucket table where any saturated gold is "Amber".

I had a perfectly good source of truth — the actual pixels I was about to send — and instead I sent a lossy human label derived from them. The model did exactly what I asked.

Fix: measure the mean colour from the cutout being transmitted and state it as sRGB. Lesson: when you have the data, don't send your summary of the data.

The blur that reached across the silhouette

Occlusion repair painted a black dress in mauve. The inpainting blur was sampling neighbours without checking whether they were inside the garment — 65% of each repaired pixel came from skin outside the cutout.

Fix: a weight-normalised blur confined to the filled region. Colour gap 106 → 18; bright outliers 175 → 1.

Lesson: a blur is an average, and an average over the wrong population is confidently wrong.

My test measured the wrong thing and passed

I wrote an "enclosure" check for occlusion notches. It reported 0.07–0.13 and I spent an hour tuning thresholds against it before realising the marked pixels were a sliver inside a much larger notch. The metric was meaningless.

Worse: a probe once reported "8 passed, 0 failed" while having aborted part-way. A suite that throws records zero failures and a short count — which reads exactly like success.

Fix: the runner now treats an aborted probe as a failure. And when the server's core-logic suite passed 80/80 on its first run, I didn't celebrate — I wrote a mutation test that breaks that logic nine different ways in memory and requires each fault to be caught. All nine were.

Lesson: "it passed immediately" is evidence about your test, not only your code.

"chic" on 100% of garments

My tagger was mechanical, and it put chic on every single garment and boutique on 61% of them. Words in no occasion list and no real search, occupying two of five tag slots.

Worse, it inferred fabric from colour: the colour describer returns "Smooth Twill" for anything black, so a silk camisole and leather boots were both tagged twill.

Fix: a vision model does tagging, handed the stylist's own vocabulary to choose from, with the filler words explicitly banned. The engine may now only contribute words it actually measured from texture. chic: 100% → 0.

"gym workout" was an office outfit

The stylist matched keywords by substring. So workout contains work → office. And travel contains rave → "airport travel day" came back as a party outfit.

Fix: whole-word matching, plus an explicit precedence list rather than relying on declaration order. Bucket accuracy went 17/22 → 22/22, with all 71 servable slots deterministic.

Lesson: substring matching on natural language is a bug with a delay fuse.

How it's tested

460 assertions across 21 headless-Chrome suites, driving the real application rather than mocks. No node, no npm, no Jest — a PowerShell HTTP server plus headless Chrome, with each page POSTing its results back to disk.

Highlights:

  • Cutout quality on 10 fixtures, three of them white-on-white
  • Occlusion repair measured with the repair on and off in the same run, so the comparison is between two measurements rather than against a remembered number
  • What the AI prompts actually say — because model output isn't reproducible, but prompt text is
  • Render-fidelity rejection in both directions
  • Tag distribution across the whole catalogue
  • Stylist bucket accuracy and per-slot determinism
  • The mutation test described above

What I deliberately did not build

An honest boundary is more useful than an implied one.

  • It doesn't put clothes on you. There's a mannequin, not a body model.
  • It doesn't simulate drape. See step 8.
  • It doesn't fill in a garment's neckline. A neckline and an occlusion bite are indistinguishable at pixel level, and turning a V-neck into a boat neck is worse than leaving a notch.
  • No accounts, no sync. The wardrobe lives in one browser. That's a real limitation, not a philosophy.
  • Model behaviour isn't regression-tested. Every check costs a call and the same prompt doesn't return the same picture twice. So the prompts are tested for what they say, and each render is verified on arrival instead.

The thing I keep coming back to

Four of my five worst bugs were my code lying to itself: a label promoted to truth, an average over the wrong pixels, a metric measuring nothing, a test passing on zero.

The AI was never the unreliable part. It did what it was asked, every time. The unreliable part was the code deciding what to ask — and, more than anything, the code deciding whether the answer was good.

If you build with generative models, budget most of your effort for the verification layer. Not the prompt. The prompt is the easy half.
